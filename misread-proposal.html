MISREAD

When the system names the wrong thing, reality starts to agree.

The player works as a visual auditor inside a damaged digital archive. An AI assistant labels everyday drawings. At first it is accurate. Then it becomes confidently wrong, and the images begin changing to match its mistakes. The player must observe carefully, challenge false labels, and protect the original record.

01

Played case study

Quick, Draw!

20SECONDS
⌁

Quick, Draw! is a browser game in which a neural network tries to recognize a player’s doodle before a 20-second timer ends. The project was created by Jonas Jongejan, Henry Rowley, Takashi Kawashima, Jongmin Kim, Nick Fox-Gieg, and collaborators at Google Creative Lab and the Data Arts Team.

The project later became a public dataset of 50 million drawings in 345 categories. That scale matters because the game turns many different human ways of drawing the same object into training material.

Three meaningful moments

01

The timer starts

The prompt and countdown create pressure immediately. There is almost no learning period.

02

The machine guesses aloud

Rapid guesses make the model feel present. Wrong guesses reveal how differently a person and a system can read the same lines.

03

The results reveal patterns

After the rounds, the player can compare a drawing with other examples. A private doodle becomes part of a larger visual language.

Player-action events

  1. 01Start the game.
  2. 02Read the drawing prompt.
  3. 03Draw with the pointer or finger while the timer runs.
  4. 04React to the network’s changing guesses.
  5. 05Continue after a correct guess or timeout.
  6. 06Repeat the cycle across six prompts.
  7. 07Open a result to compare the doodle with similar drawings.

02

Critical discussion with AI

What the first analysis missed

Verified research

The game is a data-collection system too.

The official dataset documents 50 million drawings across 345 categories. Drawings include time-based stroke data and metadata such as whether the game recognized them.

Gap 01

Wrong guesses are part of the experience.

The first AI analysis focused on speed and successful recognition. It missed that incorrect guesses are the moment when players notice the model’s limits.

Gap 02

Learning was described too casually.

The AI implied that one player’s session changes the model immediately. The official sources describe contributed drawings and a growing dataset, but they do not confirm a personal real-time update.

AI

“The strongest lesson is that fast feedback makes the system feel intelligent.”

ME

That is only half of it. The wrong guesses are more interesting because they expose the gap between what I drew and what the system believes.

AI

“Then incorrect labels could work as failure states.”

ME

I do not want them to feel like simple failures. In my game, a wrong label changes the world and asks the player to defend their own observation.

My conclusion + design decision

Hallucination becomes meaningful when a confident mistake affects what the player sees next. MISREAD will turn wrong labels into visual changes. The player’s main skill is careful observation, not speed.

03

Narrative and concept

A mistake that edits reality

Hallucination

Confident false perception

I interpret hallucination as a system seeing something that is not there, then presenting the mistake with enough confidence that other people may accept it.

Game world

A damaged visual archive

The archive stores simple drawings of everyday objects. Its automated labeler is failing, and each false label slowly rewrites the stored image.

Player role

The last visual auditor

The player compares the original evidence with the AI label, challenges mistakes, and restores the record before it disappears.

Desired experience

Calm → doubt → agency

The game begins clean and understandable. It becomes uncertain, then ends by showing that the player can question a convincing system.

05

Style and sound guide

Friendly interface, unstable evidence

Color

ArchiveSystemConfidenceConflictVerified

The colors begin soft and organized. Coral appears when the image and label disagree.

Interface

SYSTEM / 98%Is this what you saw?

Avenir-style sans serif keeps the page connected to the portfolio. Monospaced labels separate the system voice from the player’s voice.

Motion

CUPLAMP

Labels drift out of alignment, duplicate, and settle into the wrong word. Motion stays short so the player can still study the evidence.

Sound

  • Soft click — choice accepted
  • Two-note detune — contradiction
  • Low archive hum — rising uncertainty
  • Pencil texture — visual evidence appears

The browser will generate simple tones. The spoken, immediate feedback of Quick, Draw! is the main interaction reference.

Reference 01: Quick, Draw! by Google Creative Lab — rapid prompts, minimal drawing surface, and immediate machine feedback.

Reference 02: The Quick, Draw! Data — grids of varied human drawings inspired the visual-archive setting.

Original visuals: Interface mockups, flowchart, storyboard, and object icons on this page were created for MISREAD.

06

Two-week build plan

Small enough to finish, clear enough to test

WEEK 2

Build the playable prototype

  • Create the opening and three short stages.
  • Build the drawing-card and label system.
  • Add Trust and Challenge choices.
  • Track decisions and show an ending.
  • Add responsive controls, motion, and simple browser-generated sound.

Prototype goal: one complete 3–5 minute playthrough.

WEEK 3

Test, refine, and publish

  • Test with at least three people.
  • Check whether the goal and choices are understandable.
  • Adjust pacing, label confidence, and visual distortion.
  • Check phone layout, keyboard use, contrast, and reduced motion.
  • Fix bugs and publish the final game from this portfolio.

Testing question: When did you stop trusting the system, and why?
