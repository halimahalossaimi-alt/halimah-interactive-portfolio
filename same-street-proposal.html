Hallucination · Week 1 proposal

SAME
STREET

You escaped the monster. Now run the same street as the monster.

HUMAN LOOP · PANIC ESCAPE THEY ARE GETTING CLOSER

Same Street is a short looping runner set in a cute village. The player first escapes a group of zombies, then repeats the same route as a zombie chasing their former character. The street and controls stay familiar, but color, sound, camera, and interface change the emotional meaning from panic to excitement. A final mixed loop makes both versions unstable.

01

Played case study

Canabalt

1BUTTON
➜

Canabalt is an automatic runner created by Adam Saltsman, with music by Danny Baranowsky. The current browser version was ported to HTML5 by Cameron Taylor. The player uses one button to jump while the character runs through a collapsing city.

I chose it because it creates tension with a very small control system. Its automatic movement, jump timing, distance counter, environmental motion, and fast restart helped me think about how one repeated route could feel different across multiple loops.

Five player-action events

Start the gameThe character runs automatically.Direction and speed do not need extra controls.
Press jumpThe character jumps over a small obstacle.One action can carry the whole game.
Time a longer jumpThe character clears a rooftop gap.Timing changes the risk even when the input stays the same.
Hit a wall or miss a roofThe run ends immediately.The world is easy to read, but mistakes still matter.

Case-study takeaway

I will keep one main jump action and a fast restart. My new idea is to make the repeated run part of the story: each loop changes who the player is and how the same street feels.

02

Critical discussion with AI

From a runner to a change in perspective

Verified research

The simple control was intentional.

Official credits identify Adam Saltsman as the designer, and a published interview describes his goal of making a fun one-button game. The official version also presents the experience as an escape through a collapsing city.

Gap 01

Speed was only part of the experience.

The first AI analysis focused on reaction time. My play showed that birds, background destruction, distance, and music also make the run feel urgent.

Gap 02

Restarting was treated only as failure.

The quick restart creates a repeated cycle. I challenged the idea that repetition was only a score mechanic and used it as the narrative structure for my game.

AI

“The main lesson from Canabalt is that speed and one-button jumping can create tension.”

ME

That does not fully explain my idea. I want the character to return to the same place. One loop you are running from the zombies, and the next loop you are the zombie.

AI

“Then repetition can become part of the story. Changing roles can make the player question who is dangerous.”

ME

When you are running away, the world should feel panicked. When you are the zombie, the same world should feel like attack mode and excitement.

My conclusion + design decision

I decided to keep the route and jump control consistent while changing the player’s identity and emotional feedback. The same street becomes frightening, exciting, and uncertain depending on who is looking.

03

Narrative and concept

The monster changes with the viewer

Hallucination

One place, conflicting realities

I interpret Hallucination as an experience where emotion changes what feels real. Panic makes the village look threatening. Excitement makes the same chase feel powerful.

Game world

A cheerful village caught in a loop

Rounded houses, gardens, fences, signs, and a pond create a welcoming world. Each return changes its color, sound, and meaning.

Player role

Runner, zombie, then both

The player first escapes, then chases their earlier character, and finally changes between both roles while moving through the same street.

Desired experience

Panic → excitement → uncertainty

The player learns a simple action, enjoys the role reversal, and then questions which version of the street is real.

A character enters a cheerful village and notices zombies following them. They run through the village, jumping over fences, garden tools, and fallen signs. At the edge of town, the screen glitches and returns them to the same starting place.

On the second loop, the player is a zombie chasing the character they previously controlled. The same route feels powerful and exciting. During the final loop, the game switches between both roles. At the pond, the two reflections overlap and the player chooses to run again or stop the loop.

Controls and rules

Jump

Press Space, click, or tap. The same input works in every loop.

Automatic movement

The current character runs forward without directional controls.

Human goal

Clear obstacles and keep distance from the zombies.

Zombie goal

Clear obstacles and close the distance to the human.

Failure

A collision restarts the current loop quickly.

Access

Sound can be muted. Visual labels always explain the current role.

05

Style and sound guide

Cozy village. Unstable point of view.

HUMAN LOOP

Panic and escape

  • Muted peach, blue, and gray
  • Closer camera and narrow view
  • Fast heartbeat and nervous breathing
  • Warning text: “ESCAPE”
  • Small shake after a near miss
ZOMBIE LOOP

Attack mode and excitement

  • Bright green, purple, orange, and red
  • Lower, wider camera
  • Heavy drums and energetic bass
  • Goal text: “CATCH THEM”
  • Bold speed lines after a clean jump
FINAL LOOP

Uncertainty

  • Both palettes interrupt each other
  • Role label changes during the run
  • Heartbeat and drums overlap
  • Buildings briefly show two versions
  • Reduced-motion mode uses color and labels

Color

VillageCalmPanicZombieAttack

Color communicates emotional perspective. Text labels and shapes support the colors so the difference remains understandable.

Interface

ROLE: HUMANESCAPEROLE: ZOMBIECATCH THEM

Large role and goal labels change at every loop. The jump instruction stays in the same place.

Motion

➜

Layers of houses, trees, and fences move at different speeds. Short impact motion confirms jumps and near misses. A reduced-motion option removes camera shake.

Sound

  • Human — heartbeat, quick footsteps, breathing
  • Zombie — heavy drums, bass pulse, growl
  • Reset — reversed bell and short silence
  • Ending — both themes slow and merge

Sound begins only after the player starts. A persistent mute button remains visible, and every important cue also has a visual response.

Visual reference: Animal Crossing: New Horizons by Nintendo — a reference for a welcoming village, rounded forms, readable characters, and warm colors. Same Street will use original characters and scenery.

Interaction and motion reference: Canabalt by Adam Saltsman / Finji — automatic running, one-button jumping, environmental movement, and fast restarts.

Sound reference: Canabalt’s music by Danny Baranowsky demonstrates how rhythm can strengthen forward motion. Same Street will use original or browser-generated cues for panic, attack mode, reset, and resolution.

Original proposal visuals: The hero illustration, flowchart, storyboard, interface samples, and color system on this page were created for Same Street.

06

Two-week build plan

One route, three emotional versions

WEEK 2

Build the playable prototype

  • Create one side-scrolling village route.
  • Build automatic running and one jump action.
  • Add obstacles, collision, and quick restart.
  • Create the human and zombie loops.
  • Add the mixed final loop and ending choice.
  • Add responsive touch and keyboard controls.

Prototype goal: one complete 3–5 minute playthrough using original simple shapes and characters.

WEEK 3

Test, refine, and publish

  • Test with at least three players.
  • Check whether the role change is clear without explanation.
  • Compare whether the human loop feels panicked and the zombie loop feels exciting.
  • Adjust jump timing, chase distance, sound, and pacing.
  • Check phone controls, keyboard focus, contrast, mute, and reduced motion.
  • Fix bugs and publish the final game.

Testing question: When did the same street begin to feel different, and what caused that change?
